All work Settly · 2024 - Now

Three connected platforms, one relocation journey.

I lead design across Settly's B2B2C relocation product: a client platform for HR teams, an internal platform for the hosts who run each move, and a mobile and web app for the employees relocating. Each platform is built for its own user, but they work as one system. Three projects show how that work cut manual effort, halved vendor response times and more than doubled mobile adoption.

Context

Three platforms, each built for its user, working as one.

Each of Settly's three audiences has its own platform, designed around what they need to do. Behind the scenes they are connected: when a client creates a new case, it is sent to the host platform, and hosts then guide and message the relocating employee through the employee app. A change in one platform affects the others, so every design decision had to work across the whole system.

Client platform · HR teams

HR teams create new relocation cases, upload documents and action requests for their company's moves.

Host platform · Settly operations

Hosts receive new cases, run each relocation and coordinate employees and vendors.

Employee app · Mobile & web

Relocating employees follow their move, complete tasks and get help through a stressful process.

My role

Leading design end to end, across all three platforms.

I work in a cross-functional team with a product manager and engineers. I own the design process from discovery and research through prototyping, usability testing and delivery, and use Mixpanel data alongside interviews to find problems and measure results.

Constraints

Limited time and technical constraints.

We had to work within tight timelines and what was technically possible. That shaped how we worked: we split large problems into phases, tested early with hosts and users, and brought engineers into ideation so designs stayed buildable.

01 · AI-assisted host workflows

AI drafts the message. The host stays in charge.

The problem

Hosts spent most of their time messaging users, and much of it was repetitive. Templates helped, but hosts still had to find the right one, then personalise and adapt it. Fully automated replies weren't the answer either: sensitive relocation cases need human judgement.

How we found it

Mixpanel tracked how many messages hosts sent and how long each took, and showed that messaging took up most of their time. Reading the messages themselves, we found hosts often repeated the same information to different users. We then ran sessions with hosts to learn what they struggled with and which questions they were asked most.

First version: three smart replies

Our first release offered hosts three suggested replies to choose from. Hosts used it, but less than we hoped. Follow-up interviews showed three reasons why:

Tone

Every host has their own style of writing, and the suggestions didn't sound like them.

Accuracy

Some suggestions weren't good or accurate enough to send.

Effort

Reading and comparing three options added time and effort instead of saving it.

Iterating to one draft

Over several rounds of interviews and usability testing, we addressed each issue. Engineers ran evals to improve answer accuracy, we added tone selection and a source view so hosts could check the information, and we simplified three options down to one draft.

  • Three suggestions to read, compare and pick from
  • Generic tone that didn't match the host
  • Answers that weren't always accurate
  • No way to check where the information came from
  • One draft, prefilled in the message field to edit or send as is
  • Tone selection to match each host's style
  • Accuracy improved through engineering evals
  • A source view to validate every answer
  • Tab to confirm, with thumbs up or down feedback on every draft

The solution

An AI assistant writes one draft reply using details from the relocation case and places it directly in the message field. Hosts stay in control: they can send it as is, edit it, or refine it with quick actions. We later extended the same interaction to vendor communication and other operational workflows.

  • Expand
  • Shorten
  • Deep Dive
  • Tone

Key decisions

  • One draft, not three. Fewer choices meant less reading and a faster path to sending.
  • Assist, don't automate. Hosts review every message, because relocation cases are personal and sensitive.
  • Show the sources. Seeing where an answer came from helps hosts trust the draft and catch mistakes.
Impact
~30%Of host messages drafted with AI
~20%Estimated reduction in manual effort
02 · Centralised vendor communication

Vendor emails out of the inbox and into the case.

The problem

Hosts talked to relocation vendors through external email inboxes. Conversations weren't linked to cases, vendor information was hard to find, and teams couldn't easily see whether a vendor had been contacted, sent a quote or confirmed a service. Stakeholders and the operations team had flagged it as a major pain point.

Core problems

  • No single source of truth. Messages were spread across personal inboxes, shared inboxes and vendor systems, with no unified view.
  • Uncontrolled vendor behaviour. The system assumed vendors would reply to the same email alias, but nothing guided or enforced it.

How we approached it

Because we knew the problem was large, we did extensive research up front and then worked through the flow one phase at a time.

1 · Problems & goals

Defined the problems and what success should look like.

2 · Current workflow

Mapped how hosts and vendors actually communicated.

3 · Discovery questions

Drafted the questions the research had to answer.

4 · Evidence

Collected real host-vendor email threads and interviewed hosts about their pain points.

5 · Desired flow

Mapped the future workflow end to end.

6 · Phased delivery

Split the flow into phases. For each one: define the problem, run lightning demos and ideation, then get rounds of feedback from hosts and engineers before moving on.

Setting the goal: less time per case

We started by asking the team to imagine three to six months ahead: what had we achieved for the business, and what was different from today? The answers came together around one goal: less time per case. That meant centralising vendor information, automating repetitive steps, and only involving hosts in vendor and client communication when they were really needed. Centralising the information also supported the longer-term strategy of feeding it into Settly's knowledge engine for future features.

What the emails told us

We analysed real host-vendor email threads, tagging every message by type. Most emails were purely operational: requests, confirmations and changes, with very little actual decision-making. The biggest sources of waste were vendors sending information in fragments, requirements not being captured upfront, and confirmations that could have been bundled with updates.

Key finding
~85%Of emails in a temporary accommodation thread were operational
~72%Of a travel booking thread was requests, confirmations and changes

Mapping the desired flow

We mapped the future flow for each service, showing who acts at each step (user, host or vendor) and through which channel. Each flow was split into phases, such as criteria validation, vendor engagement, user approval, and booking and confirmation. We then designed and tested one phase at a time.

Challenges along the way

Many contacts per vendor

A single vendor often had several contacts, each with a different email address.

Bringing email in-house

Everything had lived outside the platform, so existing email threads had to be integrated and imported.

New email addresses

Moving to unique Settly email addresses meant clearly communicating the change to clients.

Broken threads

Vendors often started new email threads instead of replying, which made it hard to keep conversations connected to the right case.

The solution

We brought vendor search and communication into Settly's host platform. Hosts can search and filter vendors by what the case needs, email them from a case-specific address, use templates, and track every request by status.

Request statuses

Every vendor request has a clear status, so the whole team can see where things stand.

  1. Contacted
  2. Quotation requested
  3. Confirmed
Impact
34h → 17hMedian vendor response time
40%Increase in user retention
03 · User app and dashboard redesign

Answering three questions every employee has.

How it started

This project grew out of our research into host messaging. The same questions kept coming up, which told us employees weren't being informed well enough by the app. If the app were clearer and easier to use, employees could help themselves instead of messaging their host.

The problem

Employees struggled to see their overall progress, what needed their attention and what would happen next. Tasks, guidance and service information were spread across the product. The mobile app was also buggy and hard to use, so most employees used the desktop version instead.

Rethinking the whole app

We reviewed every flow and feature. Instead of fixing screens one at a time, we decided to redesign the entire app so the experience would be consistent. We grouped the messages employees sent to hosts by theme, and three questions came up again and again. They became the basis of the redesign.

Three questions

  1. What is happening?
  2. What do I need to do next?
  3. Where can I get help?

The solution

We redesigned the dashboard, checklist and key mobile and web journeys. Information now appears at the right time, based on the employee's stage of relocation, with articles relevant to that stage. Upcoming actions come first, and everything an employee needs is on their dashboard.

Testing

We tested each iteration with relocating employees and with internal staff who had little experience of the app, giving us fresh eyes on whether it was easy to understand.

Before and after

The old app was buggy and hard to use, so employees fell back on the desktop version and messaged their host for answers. The redesign reworked every key journey around what the employee needs at their current stage.

01 Home dashboard

  • Articles came first, before anything the employee had to do
  • Tasks were colour tiles with no status or context
  • No overview of progress or what was coming up
  • Updates and requested files at the top
  • Current stage with progress and the next step, such as an onboarding call
  • Upcoming events with date, time and location

02 Checklist

  • Large, bright cards that were hard to scan
  • Only the next item in each category was visible
  • No sense of overall progress
  • Overall relocation journey progress at the top
  • Expandable categories with every step in a timeline
  • Completed steps show when they were done

03 Articles to Guide

  • A long feed of large image cards
  • No search: only scrolling or tabs
  • Little information about each article
  • Search first: "How can we help you?"
  • "Your reads" with category tags and read times
  • Browse all categories for self-service

What I learned

Lessons from designing a connected system.

  • One problem can reveal another. Researching host messaging showed us why employees needed help in the first place, and that led to the app redesign.
  • More options aren't always better. Three AI suggestions created more work. One good, editable draft got used.
  • Not every planned feature earns its place. When the evidence showed that something on the roadmap wouldn't make a real difference for users, it was better to pause or shelve it early and put our time into the work that would.
  • Break big problems into phases. Working through vendor communication one phase at a time, with hosts and engineers involved throughout, kept a complex project manageable.
